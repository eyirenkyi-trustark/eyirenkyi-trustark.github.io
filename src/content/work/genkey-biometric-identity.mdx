## Context

When GenKey acquired ClaimSync I went with it, and the problem I had been working on got
considerably larger.

GenKey was founded in 2001 out of a merger with priv-ID, a spin-out of Philips' biometrics
research division, and it builds large-scale biometric identity programmes for governments
and public institutions. It is headquartered in The Hague with an office in Accra, which is
where I worked. The technology sits underneath two things that matter enormously in West
Africa: who is allowed to vote, and who is entitled to health care.

I joined in January 2014. Ghana had first deployed biometrics in its 2012 general elections,
using a system supplied by GenKey, and that deployment
[had been publicly criticised for failures](https://www.biometricupdate.com/202412/biometrics-use-in-ghana-elections-matures).
That was the environment I walked into, and it shaped what I think this work is actually
about.

## Approach

A biometric system has one failure mode that matters more than all the others, and it is not
a false accept.

It is the legitimate person who does not match. An elderly voter whose fingerprints have
worn smooth. A manual labourer with damaged ridges. Someone whose enrolment was captured
badly, in poor light, by a tired operator at the end of a long day. On a spreadsheet that is
a false reject rate. In a queue outside a polling station it is a citizen being told that the
machine does not believe they are who they say they are.

Everything I worked on came back to that. The interfaces were used by field operators
working in heat, in bad light, on constrained hardware, often far from anyone who could
help, under time pressure that was not negotiable because an election has a date. The design
question was never how to make the software impressive. It was what the operator should see
in the three seconds after a match fails, and whether the next action is obvious enough to
take under pressure with a queue forming.

That meant designing for the exception rather than the happy path, because at national scale
the exception happens tens of thousands of times. It meant capture quality feedback at
enrolment, when the problem is still cheap to fix, rather than discovering it at verification
when it is not. And it meant an escalation path that keeps a real person in the process,
because the alternative is a machine disenfranchising someone.

The same logic applied on the health side. Biometric membership verification for insurance is
a fraud control, and it works: it stops one person's card being used by several people, which
is a real and expensive problem. But the failure mode is identical. A patient who cannot
authenticate is a patient who may not be treated, and the system has to be far more careful
about that direction of error than about the other one.

## My role

I led front-end engineering and user experience for these systems.

In practice that meant building the interfaces operators used, sitting with the people who
ran deployments, and translating what went wrong in the field into changes that made the
next deployment less fragile. Coming from ClaimSync, I already knew the health insurance
side, so I worked across both the claims lineage and the identity systems.

It was also the period when I stopped being a founder who designed and became an engineer
who designed, which is a distinction that took me some time to appreciate. The standards are
different when your code runs somewhere you will never visit, in conditions you cannot
simulate, on a day that cannot be rescheduled.

## Outcome

- Delivered front-end and user experience for biometric voter registration systems used in
  African elections.
- Worked across biometric verification for health insurance, extending the claims work that
  began at ClaimSync.
- Built operator-facing interfaces designed around capture quality and failure handling
  rather than demonstration flows.

## Why it still matters

Ghana has kept going with this. By the December 2024 general elections, 18.3 million
registered voters were eligible to be verified biometrically, using both fingerprint and
face. Reporting from that election describes exactly the problem I have described above:
in some constituencies, aged voters could not be verified by fingerprint, and officials
[resorted to facial verification](https://www.biometricupdate.com/202412/biometrics-use-in-ghana-elections-matures)
to get them through. The fallback existed, and it worked.

Ghana's National Health Insurance Scheme has since moved in the same direction, rolling out
[biometric membership authentication](https://www.nhis.gov.gh/) to cut claims fraud.

I am not claiming credit for any of that. I am pointing out that a decade later the
engineering question has not changed. The system is judged on whether the person it fails
has somewhere to go.

## What I would do differently

I would have pushed much harder to get the failure data back from the field in a structured
form.

We learned about problems through stories: an operator said this, a deployment lead reported
that. The stories were true and they were useful, but they were not countable, so we could
not tell a widespread problem from a memorable one. Instrumenting capture quality and match
failure by device, by operator, by region would have told us where to spend the effort. We
were arguing from anecdote about a system that was generating an enormous amount of evidence
nobody was collecting.
