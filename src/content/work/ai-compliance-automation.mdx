## Context

Compliance reporting is an unusually good candidate for automation and an unusually bad
candidate for a chatbot.

The work was recurring, structured and expensive: gather data, reconcile it, format it,
explain it, repeat next cycle. It consumed roughly 16.7 person-days a month of effort that
produced no new insight, only the same insight re-derived by hand.

But the constraint that makes it valuable is also the constraint that makes it hard. A
compliance artefact has to be defensible. An auditor is not asking whether the output sounds
plausible. They are asking where the number came from, whether the same inputs produce the
same answer, and who is accountable for it. A system that is usually right and occasionally
inventive is worse than no system, because it launders uncertainty into an official-looking
document.

## Approach

I worked from one principle: automation can accelerate compliance only when the control
model is stronger than the manual process it replaces.

That principle does a lot of work. It rules out generation where retrieval will do. It
requires that the same question asked twice returns the same answer, because reviewers need
to compare cycles. It means unsupported scenarios must be refused rather than approximated,
since a confident guess is the one failure mode an audit cannot absorb. And it requires that
anything involving judgment routes to a human by design, not as an exception.

So the deterministic path carried the load. The language model was used where it is genuinely
strong, which is turning a verified result into clear guidance a person can act on, rather
than being asked to decide what the result was.

The sequencing mattered too. I started with the highest-friction recurring tasks, the ones
structured enough to automate honestly and costly enough to justify the control work. And I
put the capability where the work already happened, inside an existing enterprise engineering
assistant, rather than asking engineers to visit a new destination. A compliance tool nobody
opens has a compliance value of zero.

## My role

I owned the product direction, the control expectations and the rollout path. I translated
compliance requirements into product requirements, aligned the design with Responsible AI
expectations, shaped the experience and secured engineering support.

A quieter part of the job was narrative discipline. There is significant organisational
enthusiasm for anything labelled AI, and it pulls teams toward demonstrations. I kept this
framed as operational leverage with an audit trail, which is less exciting to present and
considerably more likely to survive contact with a reviewer.

## Outcome

- Recurring compliance and vulnerability reporting effort reduced about 70%, roughly 16.7
  person-days each month.
- About 90% of daily SLA reporting automated.
- AI-assisted remediation guidance shipped into an enterprise engineering assistant under
  Responsible AI controls, generating fix instructions and code changes with review paths
  intact.
- Zero PCI and FedRAMP vulnerability audit findings in H1 2025.

## What I would do differently

I would treat production hardening as a roadmap item from day one instead of a final
milestone.

The prototype proved value in weeks, which is exactly the problem. A working demo creates an
expectation of imminence, and everything that actually determines whether the system can be
trusted, which is permissions, reliability, monitoring, failure behaviour and control
documentation, sits after that demo and takes far longer. I under-planned that phase because
the early signal was so strong.

The general lesson: with AI features, the demo is the cheap part. Plan the schedule around
what it takes to make the thing defensible, not around what it takes to make it impressive.
