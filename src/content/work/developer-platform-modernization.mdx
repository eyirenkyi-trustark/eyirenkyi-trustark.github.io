## Context

Internal platforms have a distinctive problem. Your users cannot leave, and that is worse
for you than it sounds.

In a market, indifference shows up as churn and you are forced to respond. Inside a company,
indifference shows up as compliance: teams technically adopt the thing, do the minimum, and
route around it in practice. The metric looks acceptable while the product quietly fails.

The modernization platform had genuinely useful data about service health, dependency
currency and delivery performance. What it did not consistently have was relevance at the
moment of decision. Engineering teams could see information, but seeing information and
knowing what to do next are different states, and only the second one changes behaviour.

## Approach

I shifted the product from presenting data to answering a question the service owner
already had.

That sounds small and it is not. It changes what you build. A page that lists a service's
properties is a reference document. A view that says this dependency is the one exposing
you, here is the migration path, here is what it costs you to keep deferring it, is a
decision aid. Only the second earns a return visit.

We also introduced four DORA reliability metrics across 497 services, reported monthly to
180 owners. The value was less the metrics themselves than the shared vocabulary. Before,
conversations about developer experience were anecdotal and therefore unresolvable, since
every party could cite a different story. Afterwards leaders and service owners were arguing
about the same numbers, which is the precondition for actually fixing anything.

The adoption lesson was blunt. We ran awareness campaigns and they helped at the margin, but
the durable driver was relevance. Teams returned when the product made a real problem easier
to understand. They did not return because we told them about it. Internal platforms grow by
meeting developers inside work they were already doing, not by competing for their attention
with it.

Alongside this, I reduced onboarding friction by removing five duplicative tasks per service,
and converged compliance reporting so that all of a major workload's release and build data
appeared in a single audit experience instead of being reassembled from several tools.

## My role

I owned product vision, roadmap, SDLC and stakeholder communication across two major
Microsoft engineering organisations. I wrote the product artefacts, defined the customer
journeys, drove engineering prioritisation, supported user research, and positioned the
platform as a source of decision-grade data rather than another dashboard.

## Outcome

- Adoption up 82% in users and 165% in usage in a single half-year.
- Four DORA reliability metrics delivered for 497 services to 180 owners monthly.
- New-service onboarding effort reduced 16 to 22%, by removing five duplicative tasks per
  service.
- 100% base image attribution reached.
- Compliance reporting converged so 100% of a major workload's release and build data sits in
  one audit experience, retiring duplicated tooling.

## What I would do differently

I would make fewer, larger bets earlier.

In the first phase I optimised too much for coverage and for responsiveness to stakeholder
requests. Both feel like good product management. Both spread the team thin across features
that were individually reasonable and collectively unmemorable. The stronger move was to
identify the two or three journeys with the sharpest customer pain, instrument those
properly, and be willing to disappoint people about the rest.

Saying no to an internal stakeholder is harder than saying no to a market, because the
stakeholder is in your next meeting. That is a reason to plan for it, not a reason to avoid
it.
