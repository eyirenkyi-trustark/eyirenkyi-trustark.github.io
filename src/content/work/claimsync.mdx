## Context

Health care providers and insurers in Ghana were running on paper.

A claim began as handwriting, moved physically between a clinic and an insurer, and was
adjudicated by people reading documents. The consequences were predictable and compounding.
Reimbursement was slow, which strained provider cash flow. Administration consumed clinical
staff time. And fraud was difficult to detect, not because anyone lacked suspicion, but
because paper does not support the kind of pattern comparison that makes fraud visible. An
individual claim can look entirely reasonable. The pattern across a thousand claims is where
the problem lives, and nobody could see the pattern.

## Approach

We built an electronic medical record and automated claims platform from nothing, for a
market with real infrastructure constraints and very little appetite for systems that
assumed ideal conditions.

The central design tension was that the product had three audiences with different incentives.
Clinical staff needed it fast enough not to slow down care, and they were the ones doing the
data entry. Administrators needed it to shorten the reimbursement cycle. Insurers needed
output they could trust enough to pay against. If we optimised for the insurer, clinicians
would not use it, and without clinician use there was no data for the insurer to trust. The
data quality that made fraud detection possible was entirely downstream of whether the person
at the point of care found the system tolerable.

Structuring claims digitally was what changed the economics. Once claims were consistent and
machine-readable, validation could happen at submission rather than weeks later, errors could
be caught before they propagated, and patterns that should not pass unchecked became visible.
That contributed to roughly a 20% reduction in fraud.

This was where I learned the thing I have relied on ever since: in a regulated product, trust
is not a quality attribute layered on top of the features. It is the product. Everything else
is delivery mechanism.

## My role

I co-founded the company and led product design and implementation.

At that stage there is no meaningful division of labour. I sat with customers, translated
clinical and administrative workflows into requirements, designed the interface, supported
deployments and handled the problems that surfaced afterwards. I also made the resource
tradeoffs that founders make, usually with worse information than I would have liked.

## Outcome

- Built and deployed an electronic medical record and automated insurance claims platform.
- Claims automation contributed to a roughly 20% reduction in fraud.
- The company was acquired by GenKey, where I went on to lead front-end engineering and user
  experience for voter registration systems used in African elections.
- Gained founder-level experience across discovery, delivery, implementation and regulated
  operations.

## What happened next

ClaimSync came out of [MEST](https://meltwater.org/) in Accra, the Meltwater Entrepreneurial
School of Technology, where I spent two years being taught software, business and the
unglamorous discipline of selling something before you have finished building it.

The acquisition is part of why that programme is cited the way it is. When TechCrunch wrote
about MEST's expansion across Africa in 2017, it named two companies that had gone on to be
acquired, and
[ClaimSync was one of them](https://techcrunch.com/2017/09/11/1538559/). MEST has since
expanded to Lagos, Cape Town, Nairobi and Abidjan.

I mention it because founder outcomes in West Africa in 2013 were not common, and because
the institution that made it possible deserves the credit. I did not have a network. I had
a training programme that took a biochemistry graduate seriously.

## What I would do differently

I would have documented implementation patterns from the first deployment rather than the
fifth.

Early founder work rewards speed, and we were fast. The cost showed up as soon as we had
several customers live, because we could no longer easily distinguish between a genuine
customer-specific need and something we had simply happened to build differently that time.
Without that distinction you cannot tell what should become a standard product capability,
and every new deployment inherits a little more accidental variation.

Writing it down felt like overhead at three customers. It would have paid for itself by six.
