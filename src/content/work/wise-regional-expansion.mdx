## Context

Cross-border payments is a domain where the product manager does not control most of the
critical path.

I joined Wise to expand the product across the Middle East and Africa. Africa was the harder
and more interesting half of that brief, for a reason that is easy to state and hard to solve.
Remittances into Sub-Saharan Africa are among the most expensive in the world. The people
paying those fees are usually not wealthy, and the money is usually not discretionary. It is
school fees, rent, medicine, a funeral. Someone is waiting for it.

Every market had its own combination of regulation, partner readiness, payment rails, currency
controls, customer expectations and operational load, and those elements were not independent.
The licence determined what the product was permitted to do. The partner determined what it
could technically do. The local rail determined what customers would accept as reasonable
speed. Customer expectations were set by whatever they already used, which was usually cash
through an agent.

You cannot sequence that work as a normal roadmap. The dependencies do not respect your sprint
boundaries and several of them are owned by regulators.

## Approach

I treated launch readiness as the product, and sequencing as the core product judgment. In
practice the African work was three different jobs that people tend to lump together.

### Opening new corridors: Botswana and Zimbabwe

These were greenfield. A new corridor is not a feature flag. It is a payout partner, a
settlement path, a compliance posture, a pricing decision, a support runbook and a set of
customer expectations you have never tested before, all landing at once.

Botswana and Zimbabwe were opposite problems wearing the same shape.

Botswana is a comparatively orderly market. Stable currency, functioning banking system,
smaller population. The constraint was economics rather than disorder. A corridor that size
does not, on day one, justify anyone's integration effort. The argument for opening it anyway
is that the people sending into it are being served badly and charged heavily, and that a
corridor compounds once it exists.

Zimbabwe was where the case for low-cost transfers is most obvious and the operating
conditions are hardest. Currency regime, settlement reliability and the gap between official
and street value are not footnotes there. They decide whether the product is honest. My
position was that we would only open a route we could describe truthfully to a customer,
including what they would receive, in what currency, and by when. A remittance product that
is vague about the landing amount is not a cheaper product. It is a worse one with better
marketing.

For both, the discipline was the same. Pick the route you can stand behind, describe it
plainly, and refuse to ship a corridor that technically functions but fails unpredictably. A
payout that silently fails does more damage than a corridor you never offered, because the
customer has already told someone the money is coming.

### Keeping Nigeria compliant while it grew

Nigeria is the largest remittance market in Sub-Saharan Africa and the one most likely to
punish you for treating compliance as a launch gate rather than an operating discipline.

This was less visible than the launches and more consequential. A live corridor drifts.
Regulation moves, partners change their controls, volume grows into thresholds that did not
apply last quarter, and fraud adapts faster than roadmaps do. The job was to keep Nigeria
demonstrably compliant and operating cleanly while it kept growing, rather than finding a gap
during an audit or, worse, during an incident.

Concretely that meant giving compliance requirements the same status as anything
customer-facing: knowing what the rules currently permitted, designing flows and controls to
match, keeping our obligations and our partners' in sync, and making sure the operational load
of all that scaled sub-linearly with volume. If a control only works when someone remembers to
apply it, it does not work.

Growth and compliance get framed as a trade-off. In a regulated corridor they are the same
variable. The fastest way to stop growing in Nigeria is to become non-compliant in Nigeria.

### Widening the footprint

Alongside those I worked to widen the African payout footprint generally, across bank transfer
and mobile money routes in markets including Ghana, Uganda, Tanzania and West Africa. Mobile
money matters here more than a European product instinct expects. In several of these markets
the phone is the account. Designing for the bank rail first and treating mobile money as a
fallback gets the priority backwards.

The Middle East half of the brief ran in parallel and taught the same lesson from the other
end. Send Money in the UAE was not principally an engineering problem. It needed regulatory
permission before it could scale, which meant coordinating around an ADGM Money Services
licence, building operational readiness alongside it, and designing the send experience for
personal and business customers within what the licence actually permitted. Launching early
without that foundation would not have been speed. It would have been a product that had to be
withdrawn. Turkey, where we delivered instant transfers and IBAN receive, was closer to a pure
rails problem.

The gap between the two is the whole argument. The FSRA licence came in October 2019. The
product went live in April 2020, six months later, and by then
[15,000 people had already registered](https://www.thenationalnews.com/business/fintech-unicorn-transferwise-comes-to-the-uae-1.1006073)
to be told when their route opened. That waiting list is the clearest evidence I have ever
seen that the constraint was never demand. Dh169.2 billion left the UAE in 2018, the second
largest remittance outflow in the world after the United States, and until that morning none
of it could be sent fully online. The launch opened more than 80 destination countries at
the mid-market rate, and independent comparison put it at up to two times cheaper than the
banks and exchange houses people were using instead. Abu Dhabi then became the base for the
rest of the region.

## My role

I led cross-functional execution for regional expansion, coordinating product, engineering,
compliance, operations and external partners across these markets. I set launch scope, made
the sequencing calls, unblocked integration paths, and kept the team measuring progress by
customer outcomes rather than internal milestones.

Much of the real work was translation. Compliance, partner banks, mobile money operators and
engineering each held a legitimate model of the problem and none of them shared a vocabulary.
Getting those models onto one plan was the job more often than any individual feature decision
was.

I also pushed back on the idea that African corridors were a lower-tier roadmap item because
individual volumes looked small next to European ones. For a low-cost provider, fee burden per
customer is a better measure of where you create value than absolute corridor size. By that
measure these were among the highest-value markets Wise could serve.

## Outcome

- Opened new payout corridors into **Botswana** and **Zimbabwe**.
- Kept **Nigeria**, the largest African corridor, operating compliantly through growth.
- Widened the African payout footprint across bank transfer and mobile money routes, including
  Ghana, Uganda, Tanzania and West Africa.
- Launched Wise Send Money in the **UAE**, enabled by securing an ADGM Money Services licence.
  The launch was covered by
  [The National](https://www.thenationalnews.com/business/fintech-unicorn-transferwise-comes-to-the-uae-1.1006073)
  and [Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/business/transferwise-launches-online-money-transfer-in-uae-1.70988604),
  and opened more than 80 destination countries on day one.
- Delivered **Turkey** instant transfers and IBAN receive.
- Expanded access to lower-cost transfers in a period when remittances mattered acutely to
  receiving households.

## What I would do differently

I would have built a reusable market-entry playbook after the second launch instead of
carrying the pattern in my head.

Every country was genuinely different and I over-indexed on that difference. The same
questions recurred every time. What does the licence permit. Is the partner ready. How good is
the rail. What is the currency and settlement risk. What must the customer be told. What
controls apply. What does support absorb. Treating each launch as bespoke made them slower than
they needed to be, and made it harder to compare them afterwards, which is how you learn
whether a market was hard or just handled badly.

Uniqueness is real. It is also a convenient excuse not to systematise.

I would also have separated the two scoreboards earlier. Launches are visible and compliance
maintenance is not, so launches attract attention and resourcing while keeping a live corridor
clean is treated as overhead. It is not overhead. It is the thing that lets the launches keep
counting.
