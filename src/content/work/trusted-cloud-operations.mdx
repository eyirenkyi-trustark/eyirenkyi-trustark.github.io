## Context

Remediating vulnerabilities across more than 100 cloud services is not primarily a security
problem. It is a coordination problem wearing a security costume.

The estate had the scanners, the policies and the deadlines. What it did not reliably have
was an answer to three unglamorous questions: who owns this service, does the owner know
what they are supposed to do, and is the fleet actually moving toward compliance or just
generating reports about it.

When those questions are unanswered, the failure mode is predictable. Findings go to the
wrong team or to no team. Owners receive broad reminders instead of specific actions, so
they learn to ignore them. Status gets assembled by hand near the deadline, which means
nobody knows the real position until it is too late to change it. Audit exposure is the
visible symptom. The underlying condition is that the system has no memory and no
mechanism.

## Approach

I treated this as a product and operating system problem rather than a reporting one.

The first move was ownership, because everything downstream depends on it. A finding sent
to an unidentified owner is not a finding, it is noise with a deadline attached. Closing
attribution gaps meant integrating inventory data so that every service resolved to a team
that could actually act, and so the action reaching them was precise enough to be worth
their attention.

The second was cadence. Status that is assembled manually at the end of a cycle is a
retrospective, not a control. I moved the programme onto a daily operating rhythm with
automated reporting, so the position was continuously visible rather than periodically
reconstructed. That changes the character of the conversation. Leadership stops asking
where are we and starts asking what is blocked.

The third was noise. There is a real tension here and it is worth being honest about it.
Fewer, better-grouped work items make remediation tractable for the engineers doing it, but
grouping can be used to hide exposure. The rule I held to was that consolidation had to
reduce effort without reducing visibility: the audit trail stays intact, the underlying
findings remain individually accountable, and the grouping exists to make the work
actionable rather than to make the dashboard look calmer.

We consolidated 44 service namespaces into 8 repository work items on that basis. Cycle time
fell from 24 days to 7.

## My role

I directed remediation across partner engineering teams and owned the mechanism end to end:
the operating cadence, the reporting model, the attribution work, the incident workflow and
the communication to leadership.

I also stayed hands-on with the automation and the status views. That was deliberate. A
programme like this degrades the moment it depends on a person manually holding it together,
and the fastest way to know whether a mechanism is genuinely self-sustaining is to have built
the part that sustains it.

When a deployment and identity setback hit, I led a 129-incident post-mortem and converted
the findings into permanent changes: image validation, monitoring, alerting and clearer
escalation thresholds.

## Outcome

- Zero PCI and FedRAMP vulnerability audit findings in H1 2025.
- A 10-month SLA compliance streak.
- 100% service ownership attribution.
- About 80% of incident creation and about 90% of daily SLA reporting automated.
- Projected incidents reduced 35.8%, from 106 to 68, with full coverage accuracy retained.
- Remediation cycle time compressed from 24 days to 7.

## What I would do differently

I would set escalation thresholds at the start of a cycle rather than discovering them
during one.

The instinct when you own the numbers is to wait until they are clean before raising a
problem. It feels like rigour. It is actually a delay, and it trains partner teams to
believe that silence means everything is fine. Trust turned out to be built by surfacing an
accurate early signal with its uncertainty stated, not by producing a perfect number late.

I would also build contingency into the cycle explicitly, particularly where deployment
timing and infrastructure dependencies can move underneath you near the end.
