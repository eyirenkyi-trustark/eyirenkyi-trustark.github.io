## Context

A stock-out is a supply chain event. To the person standing at the counter it is a
medical one.

Independent pharmacies across West and East Africa were running on paper, intuition and
whatever the last wholesaler happened to have in the van. Ordering was guesswork. A
pharmacist who guessed low sent a patient away empty handed. A pharmacist who guessed
high tied up cash in stock that expired on the shelf. Neither mistake was visible until it
had already happened.

These are not marginal businesses. In much of the continent the community pharmacy is the
first and often the only place a person interacts with the health system. It is closer than
the clinic, cheaper than the hospital and open when both are shut. If the pharmacy does not
have the medicine, for most patients that is the end of the road, not a detour.

So the problem I was given looked like inventory software. It was actually a question about
whether a health system works.

## Approach

The instinct in this situation is to build a better ordering screen. That would have failed.

Pharmacies did not lack an ordering interface. They lacked confidence about what to order,
and they lacked the working capital to be wrong. Any tool that demanded more data entry
from an already stretched counter assistant, in exchange for a benefit that arrived weeks
later, would have been abandoned inside a month. I have watched it happen to other
products.

So I started from the day rather than the database. What does a pharmacist actually do
between opening and closing? Where does the paper live? Who touches it? What decision is
being made badly, and what would the person need to see, at the moment they are making it,
to make it better?

That reframing changed the product. Stock movement had to be a by-product of selling, not a
separate task. Replenishment signals had to arrive before the shelf was empty rather than
after. The system had to be useful on the first day, to the person using it, or it would
never survive long enough to be useful to the network.

The second decision was structural. We ran an agile delivery model across three development
hubs, which meant the team could learn from pharmacies in one market and ship the
improvement everywhere. Markets differed more than outsiders expect. Habits, infrastructure,
regulation and wholesaler behaviour all varied. The delivery model had to separate what was
genuinely local from what was common, or we would have built five products badly instead of
one well.

The third thing, and the one I underrated at the time, is that once selling is instrumented
you stop guessing about demand. A network of pharmacies running the same system produces a
picture of what patients are actually buying, at what strength, in what pack size, and in
what combination.

That last number is the argument for the whole category. A community pharmacy network with
instrumented selling is a public health sensor, and nobody set out to build one.

## My role

I defined and executed the product vision for Bloom, and I owned prioritisation and
delivery against it.

In practice that meant spending real time inside pharmacies, translating what I saw into
requirements engineers could build, and defending the small number of capabilities that
mattered against a long queue of requests that did not. I established the delivery rhythm
across the three hubs and kept the team pointed at operational impact rather than feature
count.

## Outcome

- Deployed to more than 60 independent pharmacies across 5+ African countries.
- Delivered inventory capabilities that reduced stock-outs by nearly 50% across network
  pharmacies.
- Established an agile delivery model across three development hubs.

The number I care about is the second one. Roughly half of the occasions when a patient
would have been turned away, they were not.

## What happened next

I left mPharma in November 2019, but not really. Bloom already had traction by then: live in
more than 60 pharmacies across five or more countries, with the stock-out result to show for
it. I carried on supporting the product after I moved on, through the period when mPharma
relaunched it in 2020 as the company's pharmacy operating system and scaled it hard.

What it became is the strongest argument for the original bet, so it is worth setting out.

mPharma's [2021 Impact Report](https://mpharma.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Impact-Report-_mPharma-2021.pdf)
describes Bloom as its proprietary pharmacy management software and the operating system for
community pharmacies in Africa. By the end of 2021 it reported Bloom running in over 250
pharmacies across Sub-Saharan Africa, a network of 290+ Mutti pharmacies in nine countries,
and Mutti membership above 150,000, up from 9,934 at the end of 2019. In an independent 60
Decibels survey cited in that report, more than 90% of patients said they rarely experienced
stock-outs.

By then the product had also outgrown inventory. The same report describes Bloom carrying
diagnostic test results to physicians, tracking treatment outcomes for children in a malaria
programme, supporting Covid-19 testing and following blood sugar readings for diabetic
patients. mPharma frames the ambition as a pharmacy-only electronic medical record, with
patient management as the core and inventory as a consequence.

It was also deployed in French for the Government of Gabon, to run the national central
medical stores.

The clearest picture of what the product actually did for a shopkeeper comes from Nigeria.
mPharma's [year in review of the GoodHealth Shops](https://medium.com/mpharma-insights/year-in-review-goodhealth-shops-1aad1c922be7)
describes putting Bloom on a tablet in patent and proprietary medicine vendor shops, almost
none of which had any formal way to record a sale. Owners could suddenly see daily revenue
and track stock, which mattered to them, and it made the monthly bill and the stock audit
a shared set of numbers rather than an argument. Mutti members reached 4,120 and accounted
for 65% of sales.

Then the same data did something nobody designed it for. In March 2020 a handful of shops
reported unusual demand for vitamin C and immune boosters. The dispensation data confirmed
it across the whole network, the team bought ahead against it, and the elevated share of
sales held for months. A network of corner shops had detected a behavioural response to a
pandemic. That is the argument in this case study, arriving from a direction I did not
anticipate.

The 2021 figures are mPharma's, published after my time there, and the credit for that scale
belongs to the team that ran it. I include them because they answer the question I could not
answer in 2016, which was whether tooling for small independent pharmacies was worth building
at all. A product that started as stock control for corner shops ended up carrying a national
medicines supply chain.

Nine years later I work at Microsoft, and Copilot is now being used by Zendawa to do a
version of the same job in Kenyan pharmacies: less waste, better inventory, and a credit
history built out of transaction data. I did not plan that symmetry and I cannot claim any
of it. But it does suggest the problem was real, and that it was never really about the
software.

## What I would do differently

I would have invested earlier in structured field research.

The stock-out number was real, but the sharpest insights never came from the dashboard. They
came from watching what people did when the system was wrong: how staff handled
substitutions, what they wrote on paper anyway, which numbers they quietly did not trust.
Every one of those was a product requirement in disguise, and I found them by accident
rather than by design. A deliberate field-learning cadence would have surfaced them months
sooner.

I would also have written down the distinction between local and universal much earlier.
We learned it, but we learned it per market, which is the expensive way.
