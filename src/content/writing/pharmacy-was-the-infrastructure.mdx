In 2016 I started working on software for independent pharmacies in Ghana. When I described
the job, people heard inventory management. Some of them heard it the way you hear that
someone works in insurance: a polite nod, a change of subject.

They were not wrong about what the software did. They were wrong about what it was for.

## The stock-out is not a supply chain event

Here is the situation the product existed to address. A woman walks into her neighbourhood
pharmacy with a prescription. The pharmacy does not have the medicine. She is told to come
back on Thursday, or to try somewhere else.

In a system with slack, this is an inconvenience. She goes to the next pharmacy, or the
hospital, or she orders online. In most of the markets we worked in there was no next
pharmacy within reasonable distance, the hospital was expensive and far, and there was no
online. The community pharmacy was not one channel among several. For a great many people
it was the entire encounter with the health system: closer than the clinic, cheaper than
the hospital, open when both were shut, and staffed by someone who knew their name.

So when the shelf was empty, that was frequently the end of the treatment. Not a delay. An
ending. People do not reliably come back on Thursday. They especially do not come back on
Thursday for a chronic condition that is not hurting them today.

That is why I stopped thinking of a stock-out as an operational failure. Operationally it is
a small thing, a forecasting miss, a late delivery. Clinically it is a patient who did not
get treated. The distance between those two readings of the same event is the whole reason
the software mattered.

## Why the ordering screen was the wrong product

The obvious build was a better ordering interface. We could have shipped one in a quarter.

It would have failed, and the reason is worth stating plainly, because I think it
generalises. The pharmacists were not short of a way to place orders. They were short of
confidence about what to order and short of the cash to be wrong about it. An ordering
screen addresses neither. It just relocates the guess.

Worse, it charges rent. Every internal tool demands something from the person using it:
attention, typing, a change in habit. It has to return more than it takes, and it has to
return it soon. A counter assistant serving a queue will not enter data today for a report
that helps head office next month. They will enter it for two days, then stop, and the
dataset will quietly rot while everyone upstream keeps believing in it.

So the design constraint was not "what should the pharmacist see". It was "what can we learn
without asking anyone to do anything extra". Stock movement had to fall out of selling.
Replenishment signals had to arrive before the shelf was empty, because a signal that
arrives after is just a record of a failure. The tool had to be worth using on day one to
the person actually using it, or it would never live long enough to be worth anything to
the network.

This is the part I would carry into any internal platform, in any industry. The user of the
tool and the beneficiary of the tool are often different people. When they are, adoption is
not a communications problem. It is a design problem, and the only fix is to make the tool
pay the user directly, immediately, in a currency they care about.

## Boring problems are where the leverage is

We got to roughly a 50% reduction in stock-outs across the network pharmacies, in more than
60 pharmacies across five or more countries. Half the occasions a patient would have been
sent away, they were not.

I have been in rooms where that result would be considered unglamorous. No model, no
breakthrough, no demo that makes people lean forward. Just a shelf that has the thing on it
more often than it used to.

I have come to think this is a failure of taste rather than a fact about the work. The
problems that compound are usually dull on the surface and load-bearing underneath.
Somebody has to know what is in stock. Somebody has to know who owns the service. Somebody
has to know whether the control actually ran. This work is unfashionable precisely because
it is infrastructural, and infrastructure is invisible until it is missing.

I now do a version of the same thing at Microsoft, at a different scale and in a different
vocabulary. Vulnerability remediation across a large cloud estate has a surprising amount in
common with medicine on a shelf in Accra. In both, the interesting question is not the
technology. It is whether the person who has to act can see, at the moment of the decision,
the one thing that would let them act correctly. Most of the failures I have watched in both
domains were failures of visibility and ownership long before they were failures of
capability.

## What it became

I left mPharma at the end of 2019, though I kept supporting Bloom afterwards. It already had
traction by then, live in more than 60 pharmacies across five or more countries. mPharma
relaunched it in 2020 as the company's pharmacy operating system, and what happened next
changed my mind about what we had been building.

By mPharma's own
[2021 Impact Report](https://mpharma.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Impact-Report-_mPharma-2021.pdf),
Bloom was running in over 250 pharmacies across Sub-Saharan Africa. The network had grown to
290+ pharmacies across nine countries, and membership in its Mutti programme had gone from
just under 10,000 at the end of 2019 to more than 150,000. An independent 60 Decibels survey
in that report found more than 90% of patients saying they rarely experienced stock-outs.

The software had also stopped being about stock. The report describes it carrying diagnostic
test results to physicians, tracking treatment outcomes for children in a malaria programme,
supporting Covid-19 testing, and following blood sugar readings for diabetic patients. mPharma
describes the ambition as a pharmacy-only electronic medical record: patient management as
the core, with sales and inventory as a consequence rather than the point.

And then the part I genuinely did not see coming. The same report describes Bloom deployed in
French for the Government of Gabon, running the operations of the national central medical
stores.

Those 2021 figures are mPharma's, and the credit for that scale belongs to the team running
it. I cite them because they settle a question I could not settle at the time.

In 2016 the honest version of my job was: we think tooling for small independent pharmacies
matters, and we cannot prove it. The counter-argument was always available. These are tiny
businesses. The margins are thin. Surely the leverage is in hospitals, or insurers, or
anywhere with a bigger budget line.

The answer turned out to be that the corner pharmacy was the right unit to build on, not
despite being small, but because it was where the population already was. Once something
trustworthy ran in enough of them, it could carry almost anything: a membership scheme, a
malaria programme, a diagnostic result, eventually a national supply chain. The inventory
tool was not the product. It was the reason anyone let us install anything at all.

## The lesson I actually took

Pick the problem that is closest to where the harm happens, even when it looks administrative.

Ambitious framing is cheap, and it tends to pull you toward whoever has the largest budget
rather than whoever has the most acute constraint. The useful question is narrower and less
flattering. Where does this system fail a real person, what is the smallest honest thing that
would stop it failing them, and can I make that thing good enough that people choose to keep
it?

If the answer is yes, you usually find out later that you were building infrastructure. You
just were not allowed to call it that at the time.
